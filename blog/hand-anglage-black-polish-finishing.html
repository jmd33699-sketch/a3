Hand Anglage & Black Mirror Polish

Arkansas oilstones, gentian wood pegging, and interior angles of high horology.

By Henri Vacheron, Master Horologist • Published September 2026 • Manufacture Genève Technical Archives

In the hierarchy of fine watchmaking, raw mechanical accuracy is only half the battle. A movement can meet chronometer timing standards straight from an automated CNC milling machine, yet it remains an industrial commodity—devoid of soul, art, and human presence. What elevates a movement into the exalted realm of haute horlogerie is anglage (hand chamfering and beveling) and black mirror polishing (poli noir). In our Geneva ateliers, finishing is not an afterthought; it is an uncompromising artisanal ritual that demands more bench hours than the initial assembly itself.

1. What Is Anglage? The Removal of the Industrial Edge

When a bridge, plate, or lever is cut from a sheet of German silver (maillechort) or stainless steel via high-precision CNC spark erosion (wire EDM), the resulting edges are sharp, perpendicular 90-degree corners. Under microscopic inspection, these raw edges exhibit jagged burrs, micro-cracks, and tool chatter marks that can break loose over time, contaminating the gear train lubricants.

Anglage is the craft of transforming that sharp 90-degree industrial corner into a perfectly flat, uniform, 45-degree beveled chamfer that reflects light with pristine optical clarity along the entire perimeter of the component.

While commercial luxury brands employ automated vibrating diamond-tipped CNC cutters to bevel edges, machine bevels are easily identified by trained collectors: they exhibit faint concentric machining ridges, soft rounded profiles, and a dull, hazy reflectivity. True artisanal anglage can only be executed by the human hand using traditional files and natural abrasive pastes.

"A machine can cut a thousand identical bridges in an afternoon, but only an artisan holding a piece of dried gentian wood can feel the microscopic resistance of the metal and coax it into mirror reflection."

2. The Challenge of Interior and Exterior Angles

The definitive litmus test separating hand finishing from automated machining is found in the angles of the bridge perimeter:

  • Exterior Angles (Coins Saillants): The convex corners where two bevels meet on an outward point. Hand finishing produces a crisp, razor-sharp mitre line dividing the two reflecting planes down to the apex.
  • Interior Angles (Coins Rentrants): The concave internal corners where two bevels meet within a recess. This is the absolute signature of haute horlogerie. Because rotary cutting tools have a finite circular diameter, a milling machine can never cut a sharp 90-degree or acute interior angle; it inevitably leaves a rounded fillet. An authentic interior angle must be carved entirely by hand with the triangular tip of a hand file (lime d'échappement), creating a seamless, knife-edge intersection that comes together in an imperceptible point.

A single movement such as our Calibre MH-01 features up to twenty-four hand-carved interior angles. A single slip of the file on an interior angle will permanently ruin forty hours of preceding work, consigning the entire German silver bridge to the scrap bin.

3. The Tooling: Arkansas Stones, Degussit Ceramic & Gentian Wood

The maroquinier uses awls and beeswax; the horological finisher relies on geological stones and alpine flora.

The finishing sequence follows an unhurried, multi-tiered progression:

  1. Shaping with Steel Files: Using Swiss-made Grobet needle files (cut #4 and #6), the artisan files the 45-degree angle across the edge, maintaining steady wrist elevation to prevent barrel-shaped rounding.
  2. Leveling with Natural Arkansas Oilstones: Hard natural novaculite stones quarried in the Ouachita Mountains of Arkansas are shaped into micro-wedges. Lubricated with fine oil, the stone removes file scratches and establishes an immaculate, flat geometry.
  3. Polishing with Degussit Synthetic Ceramic: Sintered aluminum oxide ceramic files refine the surface roughness down to sub-micron levels.
  4. The Gentian Wood Peg (Bois de Gentiane): The final optical mirror polish is achieved using wooden sticks harvested from the roots of yellow gentian (Gentiana lutea) that grows wild in the Swiss Jura mountains. Dried for several years, gentian wood possesses a unique cellular density that is soft enough not to scratch gold or German silver, yet fibrous enough to hold fine diamond micron pastes (down to 0.25 microns). Driven by hand across the bevel, the gentian peg polishes the metal to an incandescent, blinding mirror sheen.

4. Poli Noir (Black Mirror Polish): The Pinnacle of Flat Lapping

On the steel tourbillon carriage bridges, swan-neck regulators, and column wheel caps, our manufacture executes poli noir (black polish or specular polish)—the most demanding surface finish in metallurgy.

The steel component is affixed to a brass carrier block and rubbed in figure-eight patterns across a heavy, perfectly flat plate made of pure zinc or cast tin, charged with diamond compound or red jeweler's rouge.

The mechanical goal is absolute optical flatness. When a surface is truly flat on a nanometer scale:

  • When viewed perpendicular to the light source, the surface acts as a total mirror, reflecting 100% of incident photons directly into the viewer's eyes in a blinding burst of white light.
  • When tilted barely two degrees away from the light source, all reflected light is directed away, causing the component to absorb all ambient light and appear completely, deep, jet-black.

There are no grey middle tones, no haze, no micro-scratches. It is the ultimate manifestation of human discipline over metallurgical matter.

5. Côtes de Genève, Perlage, and the Geneva Seal (Poinçon de Genève)

Surrounding the beveled perimeters, the flat surfaces of our bridges are adorned with Côtes de Genève (Geneva stripes). These parallel, undulating wave patterns are executed using revolving boxwood laps charged with fine abrasive paste, guided by hand across the bridges with microscopic longitudinal overlap.

Beneath the bridges, hidden from casual view on the mainplate, the metal is decorated with circular graining (perlage)—overlapping circular rosettes applied one by one with spinning pegwood rods.

These traditional decorations satisfy every one of the twelve rigorous criteria of the official Poinçon de Genève (Geneva Seal). More than half of all components decorated in our atelier will never be seen by the collector unless the movement is completely disassembled during overhaul. Yet our watchmakers finish every screw head, wheel spoke, and plate pillar with identical reverence. True luxury is not that which is paraded before the public eye; it is the uncompromising beauty preserved in secret within the inner sanctum of the timepiece.

6. The Metallurgy of Maillechort (German Silver) Bridges

In mainstream watchmaking, movement plates and bridges are stamped from common CW614N brass and plated with a microscopic flash of rhodium to prevent oxidation. In the highest echelon of independent watchmaking, our manufacture rejects brass in favor of untreated Maillechort (German silver).

Maillechort is a noble non-ferrous alloy composed of 60% copper, 20% nickel, and 20% zinc. It offers profound horological advantages:

  • Warm Golden Patina: Untreated German silver possesses an intoxicating, warm, champagne-golden glow that deepens subtly over decades of aging. It requires zero electroplating, meaning the finish is pure, solid metal throughout.
  • Exceptional Structural Rigidity: With a Brinell hardness of 140 HB (compared to 90 HB for standard horological brass), German silver provides unyielding rigidity for jewel pivot bearings, maintaining axial alignment under physical shock.
  • Extreme Surface Sensitivity: Because German silver contains no protective lacquer, it is extraordinarily sensitive to moisture and oxidation. A master finisher cannot touch the bare bridge with a bare finger; a single drop of sweat will permanently stain the metal. Artisans work entirely under finger cots and laminar flow air cabinets.

7. The Ritual of Arkansas Stone Polishing and Tin Block Lapping

The physical reality of black polishing (poli noir) requires understanding the optical physics of specular reflectance. When an artisan laps a steel tourbillon carriage bar on a tin block charged with red ferric oxide (jeweler's rouge), they are performing atomic-level planar smoothing.

The tin block must be softer than the steel component being polished. This allows the diamond or iron oxide nanoparticles (typically 0.1 to 0.5 microns in diameter) to embed themselves firmly into the surface of the tin block, transforming the tin into a microscopic abrasive lap.

The artisan moves the component in gentle, rhythmic figure-eights, maintaining uniform downward finger pressure of exactly 80 to 100 grams. If the pressure tilts by even one degree, the corners of the component will round off, ruining the optical flatness. After twenty minutes of continuous lapping and ultrasonic cleaning, the surface achieves an Ra surface roughness under 0.01 microns—reflecting light with the terrifying clarity of a black hole in deep space.
