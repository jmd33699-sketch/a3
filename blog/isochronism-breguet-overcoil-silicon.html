Isochronism & Breguet Overcoil Balance

The mathematical physics of harmonic oscillation, Phillips terminal curves, and silicon physics.

By Henri Vacheron, Master Horologist • Published September 2026 • Manufacture Genève Technical Archives

In horological mechanics, the term isochronism (derived from the Greek isos meaning "equal" and chronos meaning "time") denotes the fundamental property of an oscillator whereby its period of oscillation remains strictly identical regardless of its amplitude. In simple terms: whether a balance wheel swings through a wide arc of 310 degrees (when the mainspring is fully wound) or a short arc of 220 degrees (as the mainspring approaches the end of its power reserve), each vibration must consume the exact same fraction of a second. If a balance is non-isochronous, the watch will accelerate or decelerate as it unwinds. Achieving true isochronism has occupied the greatest minds in physics—from Christiaan Huygens and Robert Hooke to Abraham-Louis Breguet and Édouard Phillips.

1. The Non-Linear Physics of the Coiled Hairspring

In an idealized harmonic oscillator, Hooke's Law dictates that restoring force ($F$) is directly proportional to displacement ($x$):

$$F = -kx$$

Under this linear assumption, amplitude does not affect period. However, a physical watch balance spring (spiral) is not an abstract mathematical equation. It is a coiled strip of metal anchored at its inner end to a collet on the balance staff and at its outer end to a stud pinned to the balance cock.

Because a standard flat Archimedean spiral is clamped asymmetrically at its outer terminal, it does not expand and contract concentrically. As the balance swings:

  • The coils on the side opposite the stud expand farther than the coils near the stud, shifting the center of gravity of the hairspring laterally by fractions of a millimeter with every vibration.
  • This shifting mass introduces a variable lateral side-thrust against the balance staff pivots, multiplying friction in the jewel bearings.
  • Consequently, as amplitude decreases from 300° to 220°, the period shifts, causing the watch to gain or lose several seconds per day.
"Isochronism is the holy grail of chronometric science: the quest to make a kinetic mechanism oscillate with the pure, unwavering constancy of celestial orbits."

2. The Breguet Overcoil and Phillips Mathematical Terminal Curves

In 1795, Abraham-Louis Breguet devised an ingenious solution that remains a hallmark of haute horlogerie: the Breguet Overcoil.

Instead of terminating the hairspring on the same flat horizontal plane, Breguet lifted the outermost coil upward and curved it back across the top of the spring toward the center, pinning it at an inner radial position.

Nearly seventy years later, in 1861, French mathematician and mining engineer Édouard Phillips published a landmark mathematical treatise demonstrating why the Breguet overcoil worked. Phillips proved that if the terminal curve of a balance spring satisfies specific center-of-gravity equations:

  1. The elastic restoring force acts strictly through the axis of the balance staff throughout the entire 360 degrees of expansion and contraction.
  2. The hairspring breathes completely concentrically, eliminating all lateral side-thrust on the balance pivots.
  3. The oscillator achieves virtually perfect isochronism across all operational amplitudes.

Forming a Breguet-Phillips overcoil is the ultimate test of a master hairspring adjuster (régleuse). Using specialized micro-tweezers under a stereo microscope, the artisan bends the ultra-fine spring wire by hand, introducing two vertical bends (the elbow) and a sweeping parabolic curve. A deviation of two microns in the curvature will completely destroy its concentric breathing.

3. Free-Sprung Balances vs. Index Regulators (Raquette)

In standard watches, timing regulation is executed via an index regulator (raquette)—a lever with two closely spaced curb pins that pinch the outer coil of the hairspring. Moving the lever changes the active vibrating length of the spring, speeding up or slowing down the rate.

While convenient for rapid servicing, an index regulator is fatal to high chronometry:

  • Curb Pin Play: The hairspring must have microscopic clearance between the curb pins to avoid sticking. As the balance oscillates, the spring bounces between the pins, causing non-isochronal perturbation.
  • Shock Vulnerability: A sharp drop or impact can displace the regulator arm, throwing the rate off by minutes per day.

At Mechanical Hour, our in-house calibres are exclusively free-sprung. There is no index regulator, no curb pins. The hairspring breathes completely unconstrained between its collet and stud.

Timing regulation is achieved through variable-inertia balances: our Gyromax-style balances feature four or eight slotted gold inertia collets set into the perimeter of the Glucydur balance rim. To adjust the rate, the watchmaker rotates opposing pairs of weights. Turning the slotted weights inward concentrates mass toward the center of rotation, reducing the moment of inertia and accelerating the rate; turning them outward slows the rate. Because the hairspring length is never touched, the isochronal curve remains immaculate for decades.

4. Silicon (Silicium) Hairsprings: The Monocrystalline Revolution

In recent years, horological materials science has witnessed its greatest leap forward since the invention of Nivarox alloy: monocrystalline silicon (Si) balance springs.

Silicon hairsprings are etched from silicon wafers using Deep Reactive-Ion Etching (DRIE) with nanometer-level precision:

  • Flawless Geometric Consistency: DRIE manufacturing allows engineers to vary the thickness of the spring coil along its length, creating integrated terminal curves that mimic the concentric breathing of a Breguet overcoil on a flat plane.
  • Thermal Stability via Silicon Oxide: Pure silicon has a positive thermo-elastic coefficient (it becomes stiffer as temperature rises). By growing an exact layer of silicon dioxide (SiO2) on the outer surface, the thermal coefficient is brought to zero, rendering the oscillator impervious to temperature swings from -10°C to +50°C.
  • Complete Antimagnetic Immunity: Silicon contains zero iron and is 100% immune to magnetic fields up to 15,000 Gauss.

5. Dynamic Poising and 6-Position Chronometer Certification

A perfect hairspring is useless if the balance wheel is unbalanced. Even a mass differential of one microgram across the balance rim will cause positional rate variations.

In our chronometry laboratory, every balance assembly is dynamically poised on ruby knife-edges and tested across six spatial positions:

  1. Dial Up (Horizontal)
  2. Dial Down (Horizontal)
  3. Crown Left (Vertical)
  4. Crown Up (Vertical)
  5. Crown Right (Vertical)
  6. Crown Down (Vertical)

The maximum allowable rate delta between all six positions must not exceed 2.0 seconds over 24 hours. Once calibrated, each Mechanical Hour movement is subjected to a 360-hour continuous chronometric trial to earn our manufacture's Observatory Rating Certificate. It is an exacting, relentless pursuit of chronometric truth—honoring the noble heritage of Swiss haute horlogerie with every beat of the balance wheel.

6. Thermal Coefficient Compensation: The Charles-Édouard Guillaume Legacy

A balance spring's frequency is heavily influenced by temperature. In early horology, temperature changes caused ordinary steel springs to expand and soften in heat, resulting in massive daily losses of up to 10 seconds per degree Celsius.

This chronometric crisis was solved by Swiss physicist Charles-Édouard Guillaume, who was awarded the 1920 Nobel Prize in Physics for his discovery of invar and elinvar (an alloy of iron, nickel, and chromium possessing an invariable elasticity modulus across normal ambient temperatures).

In our Calibre MH-06, we push thermal compensation further by pairing an elinvar-derived Nivarox-1 balance spring with an anti-magnetic Glucydur balance wheel (beryllium-bronze alloy).

The thermo-elastic expansion of the Glucydur balance wheel expands slightly in heat, increasing its moment of inertia, while the Nivarox spring becomes imperceptibly stiffer, exactly canceling the thermal expansion. The resulting thermal coefficient is less than ±0.15 seconds per day per degree Celsius—a testament to century-old metallurgical physics refined to modern perfection.

7. Dynamic Six-Position Poising on Agate Knife-Edges

Before a balance assembly is cased, it undergoes static and dynamic poising on agate knife-edge calipers (tour à pivoter).

The balance staff rests upon two parallel, razor-sharp blades of polished natural agate aligned with a spirit level. In a draft-free glass enclosure, the balance wheel is gently rolled along the agate rails.

If the wheel has the slightest heavy spot—even 0.00005 milligrams—gravity will cause the wheel to roll back and forth like a pendulum until the heavy spot settles at the lowest point. The watchmaker observes the oscillation under a 20x loupe, identifies the heavy sector, and delicately removes microscopic shavings of metal from the underside of the balance rim using a hollow carbide drill. Once properly poised, the wheel will remain completely motionless at whatever point it is placed on the rails. Only then does the movement earn its place in our Geneva chronometer registry.
